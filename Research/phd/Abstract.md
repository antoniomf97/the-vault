#phd 

Human societies are increasingly embedding AI systems into economic, institutional, and everyday processes, creating hybrid environments where humans and AI agents interact within shared systems. With increasing autonomy and operational independence, AI is shifting from being a passive instrument to an active participant in social interactions. 
These systems, as (semi-)independent actors, have effects that are no longer confined to isolated human-AI dyads but rather influence human society at scale, raising fundamental questions about their broader societal impact. Although the collective effects manifest on a large scale, understanding them requires tracing back to individual-level reasoning, specifically to the cognitive profiles that guide individual decision-making. 

In this work, we investigate how AI cognition affects the emergence, stability, and evolution of cooperation in hybrid societies of humans and AI agents. Making use of the Evolutionary Game Theory framework, we study the strategic interaction of AI agents across different levels of cognition, ranging from fixed policies to heuristic and deliberative processes, examining how these cognitive architectures differentially impact cooperative outcomes and long-term stability in hybrid societies.



**Yet population models of hybrid societies give artificial agents fixed policies or simple learning rules, and none lets the cognitive profiles of humans or agents change over time while the two remain distinct.** 

In this work, we investigate how AI cognition affects the emergence, stability, and evolution of cooperation in hybrid societies of humans and AI agents**, and whether these effects hold once the cognitive profiles of humans, and then of AI agents, change over time**. Making use of the Evolutionary Game Theory framework, we study the strategic interaction of AI agents across different levels of cognition, ranging from fixed policies to heuristic and deliberative processes, examining how these cognitive ~~architectures~~ **profiles** differentially impact cooperative outcomes and long-term stability in hybrid societies**, compared with a human-only population**. **The expected outcome is a map of the conditions under which each profile raises, lowers or leaves unchanged human cooperation, for those who design, deploy and regulate AI agents.**
