## What the field has established

The question of how artificial agents affect cooperation in hybrid societies of humans and artificial agents has received attention only recently. The literature draws on three essential bodies of work: evolutionary game-theoretic models of hybrid populations, behavioral experiments in which people interact with artificial agents, and organizational research on human-AI teams. Where studies on hybrid societies are missing, we draw on models of human-only populations.

The current state of the art suggests that artificial agents can significantly change how much humans cooperate, even when the agents follow fixed behaviors ([[sharmaSmallBotsBig2023|Sharma2023]], [[guoFacilitatingCooperationHumanagent2023|Guo2023]], [[terruchaArtCompensationHow2024|Terrucha2024]], [[zimmaroEmergenceCooperationOneshot2024|Zimmaro2024]]). However, the direction and magnitude of these effects are highly contingent on how the population is structured, on how its dynamics are set up and on how the agents are designed. We organize the literature along these three dimensions (population structure, dynamics structure and agent design), and add a fourth, cognitive evolution, which concerns whether the agents' cognitive profiles are fixed or themselves change over time.

<!--- ===== Population Structure===== -->

The population structure is the backbone of any hybrid population, as it describes who is in the population and who can meet whom. We consider three aspects: the social structure, which defines who can interact with whom; the intrinsic nature of each individual, that is, whether it is a human or an artificial agent; and the population composition, that is, the relative share of each.

<!--- Social Structure -->

First, the social structure of a population shapes its cooperation dynamics. In well-mixed populations every individual can interact with every other, whereas on networks individuals interact only with their neighbors, and the strategies favored by selection depend on that structure ([[allenEvolutionaryDynamicsAny2017|Allen2017]]). Experimental evidence supports this view, as people placed on a fixed network sustain high levels of cooperation when the benefit-to-cost ratio of cooperating exceeds the number of neighbors, while cooperation decays otherwise or when partners are reshuffled ([[randStaticNetworkStructure2014|Rand2014]]). Structure is equally relevant in hybrid populations. Learning agents raise human cooperation on lattices regardless of the imitation strength (or intensity of selection), that is, of how closely human imitation follows payoffs, whereas in well-mixed populations they do so only when imitation is weak ([[guoEngineeringOptimalCooperation2024|Guo2024]]). Unlike humans, however, language-model agents playing a repeated prisoner's dilemma cooperate more in well-mixed settings and barely adjust to network structure ([[hanStaticNetworkStructure2025|Han2025]]).

The position of artificial agents in the network is also relevant. Ten agents placed on the highest-degree nodes of a 10,000-node scale-free network shape human cooperation, and can stimulate it in prisoner's dilemma and stag hunt games, whereas the same ten agents placed at random have little effect ([[guoFacilitatingCooperationHumanagent2023|Guo2023]]).

<!---  Intrinsic Nature -->

Second, the intrinsic nature of each individual, that is, whether it is a human or an artificial agent, affects cooperation. People draw a line between humans and machines, distinguishing them by origin, autonomy, intelligence and emotional capability ([[guzmanOntologicalBoundariesHumans2020|Guzman2020]]), and they come across as less open, agreeable, extroverted and self-disclosing in first conversations with a chatbot than with a human ([[mouMediaInequalityComparing2017|Mou2017]]). Part of this effect operates through belief, since what others think an agent is matters independently of how it behaves. In a repeated prisoner's dilemma where participants were given true or false information about their partner's nature, bots were better than humans at inducing cooperation, but lost this advantage once disclosed as bots, and participants did not recover from their bias against bots over time ([[ishowo-olokoBehaviouralEvidenceTransparency2019|Ishowo-Oloko2019]]). Conversely, when identity is hidden, humans misattribute bot behavior to humans and vice versa, even when the bots are more prosocial and linguistically distinguishable ([[jiangHumansLearnPrefer2025|Jiang2025]]).

The effect of disclosure is not uniform, however. In network experiments, bots improved human coordination and cooperation even when participants knew they were interacting with bots ([[shiradoLocallyNoisyAutonomous2017|Shirado2017]], [[shiradoNetworkEngineeringUsing2020|Shirado2020]]). Evolutionary models further show that identifiability matters, as cooperation is enhanced when human-like agents who can tell whether a co-player is human or artificial disregard the AI's performance ([[zimmaroEmergenceCooperationOneshot2024|Zimmaro2024]]). Both an individual's nature and its visibility to others are therefore relevant in hybrid societies.

<!--- Population Composition -->

Third, the population composition, that is, the relative share of humans and artificial agents, must be considered. In a model where individuals play different games inside and outside their sub-population, cooperation depends on the number of sub-populations and on their relative size ([[huangEffectHeterogeneousSubpopulations2015|Huang2015]]). In hybrid populations, a minority of artificial agents can raise human cooperation, but as their proportion grows, both cooperative and defecting agents can make human cooperation disappear ([[guoFacilitatingCooperationHumanagent2023|Guo2023]]). On networks, cooperation is highest at an intermediate share of agents, because too few agents cannot protect cooperators from exploitation, whereas too many interfere with the spread of cooperation ([[fuOptimalIntegrationIntelligent2026|Fu2026]]).


<!--- ===== Dynamics Structure ===== -->

The dynamics structure describes how interactions unfold once the population is in place. We consider four components: the temporal structure, which sets when and how often individuals interact; the game-theoretic interaction protocol, which specifies the actions available to individuals and the payoffs they yield; the matching mechanism, which determines who actually interacts with whom; and the institutions, which set and enforce the external rules that constrain these interactions, for humans and artificial agents alike.

<!--- Temporal Scheduling -->

First, the temporal structure determines when and how often individuals interact. With respect to scheduling, most agent-based models advance in fixed time steps, a choice that can threaten the validity of their results and that continuous-time simulation avoids ([[kosterFastEmbeddedLanguage2024|Koster2024]]). With respect to the horizon of the interaction, whether a dilemma is one-shot or repeated changes how humans treat artificial partners. In a laboratory experiment, participants cooperated as much with an artificial agent as with a human in one-shot prisoner's dilemmas, but less in finitely repeated ones, where they were more willing to exploit an artificial partner they expected to cooperate ([[barreda-tarrazonaExploitingMachineHuman2026|Barreda-Tarrazona2026]]).

<!--- Game-Theoretic Protocols -->

Second, the game-theoretic interaction protocol, that is, the game individuals play, conditions the effect of artificial agents. The type of dilemma is the first determinant. In well-mixed populations, cooperative agents barely change the outcome of a prisoner's dilemma, where defection dominates, but facilitate cooperation in a stag hunt, a coordination game, whereas defecting agents can lead cooperation to take over in a snowdrift game, where cooperators and defectors coexist ([[guoFacilitatingCooperationHumanagent2023|Guo2023]]). The parameters of a game are as relevant as its type. On a lattice, reinforcement-learning machines stabilize cooperation when the temptation to defect is low, whereas cooperation depends mainly on the humans' strategies when the temptation is high ([[quanHumanMachineCooperation2026|Quan2026]]). The level of risk plays a comparable role in collective-risk dilemmas, where hybrid groups benefit from artificial agents mainly when the risk of collective loss is low ([[terruchaArtCompensationHow2024|Terrucha2024]]).

The structure of the interaction extends beyond pairwise symmetric dilemmas, and hybrid models have accordingly been applied to dilemmas of coordination, fairness and collective risk ([[hanSocialPhysicsAge2026|Han2026]]). In group interactions, the rule by which a collective decision is reached becomes part of the protocol. In a multiplayer ultimatum game, people make fairer proposals when more responders must accept them, and hard-coded prosocial agents sustain fairness even under lenient group decision rules ([[santosEvolutionCollectiveFairness2019|Santos2019]]). Roles may also be asymmetric. In an ultimatum game between separate proposer and receiver populations, the same fair behavior is far more effective when artificial agents act as receivers than when they act as proposers ([[songEvolutionFairnessHybrid2026|Song2026]]). Participation may furthermore be optional, in which case even bots that simply abstain can lead cooperation to dominate on lattices when imitation is strong ([[sharmaSmallBotsBig2023|Sharma2023]]). The protocol also conditions the update rules and reasoning mechanisms discussed under agent design, several of which affect cooperation differently, or even in opposite directions, depending on the class of game ([[mollemanEffectsConformismCultural2013|Molleman2013]], [[wangEvolutionaryGameDynamics2019|Wang2019]], [[pereiraCounterfactualThinkingCooperation2019|Pereira2019]]).

<!--- Matching Mechanisms -->

Third, the matching mechanism determines who actually interacts with whom. Matching should be distinguished from the social structure, since the network determines which pairs can meet, whereas the matching rule determines which pairs do. In hybrid societies, matching governs partner formation, task allocation and coalition structuring between humans and artificial agents. Rather than assuming static random mixing, recent work treats matching as an endogenous process. This view builds on evolutionary models in which cooperation prevails when individuals can adjust their social ties (Santos2006), and on network experiments in which people sever links with defectors and form new ones with cooperators (Rand2011). We organize this literature around three mechanisms, namely strategic partner selection, capability complementarity and assortative matching.

Strategic partner selection matches individuals according to what they know about potential partners, specifically how they expect them to behave. In collective-risk dilemmas involving humans and artificial agents, participants follow outcome-based rules, whereby those whose group failed prefer teams without defectors, whereas those whose group succeeded become more lenient (Santos2020). Human preferences among reinforcement-learning partners also track the agents' perceived warmth and competence, beyond their objective performance (McKee2024).

Partner choice also interacts with the identity effects discussed above. In a communication-based partner-selection game, disclosed language-model bots were initially selected less often, but gradually outcompeted human candidates as selectors learned how each partner type behaved ([[jiangHumansLearnPrefer2025|Jiang2025]]). Matching can furthermore be delegated to AI. A deep reinforcement learning social planner that recommends which ties to form or break fostered cooperation in human groups, not by isolating defectors but by embedding them in small cooperative neighborhoods (McKee2023). Autonomous agents that engineer network connections likewise increased cooperation ([[shiradoNetworkEngineeringUsing2020|Shirado2020]]).

Capability complementarity matches individuals whose different skills make the pair more productive than either would be alone. This literature measures task performance rather than cooperation. A meta-analysis finds that human-AI combinations perform, on average, worse than the better of the two alone ([[vaccaroWhenCombinationsHumans2024|Vaccaro2024]]), while newer frameworks characterize the conditions under which human-AI teams outperform both ([[gonzalezScienceHumanAI2026|Gonzalez2026]]). In assignment problems, confidence-based deferral lets the algorithm make the matching decisions it is most confident in and route the rest to humans ([[arnaiz-rodriguezHumanAIComplementarityMatching2025|Arnaiz-Rodriguez2025]]). 

Lastly, matching can be assortative, that is, dependent on the individuals' kind. It is homophilic when individuals preferentially interact with those of their own kind, and heterophilic when they favor the other kind. The initial reluctance to select disclosed bots described above is a homophilic preference of this sort, and it weakens with experience ([[jiangHumansLearnPrefer2025|Jiang2025]]). Evolutionary models examine such preferences at the population level. In a spatial prisoner's dilemma where humans and agents are each assigned preferences for interacting with their own or the other kind, cooperation improves markedly when both favor one kind over the other. Humans show the more stable prosocial behavior, while agents form highly cooperative clusters when they strongly prefer interacting with humans ([[jiaAsymmetricInteractionPreference2025|Jia2025]]).

<!--- Institutions -->

Finally, institutions are the rules, norms and beliefs that enable and constrain how actors interact, and in hybrid settings they bind humans and artificial agents alike ([[bartelheimerConceptualizingHybridIntelligent2025|Bartelheimer2025]]). In evolutionary game theory, institutions enter as rule systems that change the payoffs individuals face, and thereby whether cooperation spreads or collapses. Sanctioning institutions funded in advance outcompete peer punishment, provided that those who do not pay for them are punished as well ([[sigmundSocialLearningPromotes2010|Sigmund2010]]). An institution that rewards cooperators while they are few and switches to punishing defectors once they are many establishes full cooperation at a lower cost than rewards or penalties alone ([[chenFirstCarrotThen2015|Chen2015]]), although maximizing cooperation need not maximize social welfare ([[hanEvolutionaryMechanismsThat2024|Han2024]]). Although these results concern human populations, recent work extends them to hybrid ones. Under indirect reciprocity, artificial agents can reduce disagreement over reputations and improve cooperation (Pires2025). By contrast, rules imposed on the agents themselves, such as requiring them to always cooperate, fail to raise human cooperation ([[hintzePromotingCooperationPublic2026|Hintze2026]]). Yet most studies treat AI as a fixed-strategy participant under static, human-designed rules, and how institutions co-evolve with the humans and AI agents they govern remains open ([[hanSocialPhysicsAge2026|Han2026]]).

<!--- ===== Agent Design ===== -->

Agent design is the third dimension, and concerns what happens inside the agent. An agent's architecture dictates how it behaves, and thereby shapes the cooperative dynamics of the society it joins. It is far from a single property, as the OECD Social interaction scale describes an artificial agent through three core components of social context (embodiment, social memory, and identity) and four social skills built on them (communication, affective skills, social perception, and social problem solving) ([[oecdIntroducingOECDAI2025|OECD2025]]). Research on hybrid systems has covered these dimensions unevenly. 

<!--- Dyads and small teams -->

In dyads and small teams, where humans interact with actual systems, work has addressed how agents communicate ([[zhangInvestigatingAITeammate2023|Zhang2023]], [[crandallCooperatingMachines2018|Crandall2018]]), how they adapt their decisions to human partners ([[carrollUtilityLearningHumans2019|Carroll2019]], [[zhaoRoleAdaptationCollective2025|Zhao2025]]), how their artificial identity is perceived ([[ishowo-olokoBehaviouralEvidenceTransparency2019|Ishowo-Oloko2019]], [[karpusAlgorithmExploitationHumans2021|Karpus2021]], [[pataranutapornInfluencingHumanAI2023|Pataranutaporn2023]]), how embodied agents express emotions as members of a team (Correia2018), and how memory and identification form at the team level ([[aggarwalSelfbeliefsTransactiveMemory2025|Aggarwal2025]]). Beyond the scale's own dimensions, the same literature has examined trust ([[gliksonHumanTrustArtificial2020|Glikson2020]], [[georgantaWouldYouTrust2024|Georganta2024]], [[ulfertShapingMultidisciplinaryUnderstanding2024|Ulfert2024]]) and team cognition and composition ([[schelbleLetsThinkTogether2022|Schelble2022]], [[mcneeseWhoWhatMy2021|McNeese2021]], [[oneillHumanAutonomyTeaming2022|O'Neill2022]], [[guptaFosteringCollectiveIntelligence2025|Gupta2025]]). Some of this work already looks beyond a single scale. Team trust has been framed as a multilevel construct spanning the individual, dyadic, and team levels ([[ulfertShapingMultidisciplinaryUnderstanding2024|Ulfert2024]]). More importantly, the effects of human-AI interaction do not remain within the pair in which they occur. Exposure to AI changes how humans subsequently interact with one another, affecting their shared language, attention and mental models ([[riedlCognitiveSpilloverHuman2026|Riedl2026]]). In network experiments, bots likewise improved coordination not only for the humans connected to them but also among the humans themselves, creating cascades of benefit across the network ([[shiradoLocallyNoisyAutonomous2017|Shirado2017]]). Dyadic interactions can therefore shape outcomes at larger scales.

<!--- Large scale, hybrid society -->

Population-scale models abstract most of these dimensions away, and identity enters as the agent's kind and its visibility to others, as discussed above. What population models do represent is the agent's decision process, which we refer to as its cognitive profile, that is, how it chooses an action and how it revises that choice. The same scale orders this process by sophistication, from pre-set, scripted responses, through learning from past interactions, to inferring others' intent and weighing alternative courses of action. We follow this progression, distinguishing fixed policies, heuristic update rules, and deliberative mechanisms, and for each we note at which scale, and in which kind of population, it has been studied.

Fixed policies are the starting point of most hybrid population models, in which the artificial agents follow a rule set in advance, while the humans around them revise their strategies by imitating more successful peers. The simplest policies are unconditional, with the agent taking the same action, or cooperating with the same probability, whatever its partner does ([[sharmaSmallBotsBig2023|Sharma2023]], [[guoFacilitatingCooperationHumanagent2023|Guo2023]], [[terruchaArtCompensationHow2024|Terrucha2024]]). In prisoner's dilemma games such agents leave the payoff advantage of defection intact, and act instead by raising the chance that a human meets, and can imitate, a cooperator. Their effect therefore depends on how humans update, since cooperative bots promote cooperation when imitation is weak, whereas defection takes over when imitation follows payoffs closely ([[sharmaSmallBotsBig2023|Sharma2023]], [[guoFacilitatingCooperationHumanagent2023|Guo2023]]). In a multiplayer ultimatum game, a small number of hard-coded prosocial agents is enough to spread fair behavior far beyond the agents' own share of the population ([[santosEvolutionCollectiveFairness2019|Santos2019]]). Agents that cooperate by design can also displace human effort. In collective-risk dilemmas humans compensate for what the agents do. Under high risk, they stop contributing once cooperative agents already secure the target, and contribute more when the agents fall short ([[terruchaArtCompensationHow2024|Terrucha2024]]).

Other policies are conditional, in that the rule is still fixed but the action it prescribes depends on the partner's behavior. This requires the agent to recognize what its partner does or intends, a capacity these models assume rather than represent. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, Samaritan AI, which cooperates with everyone, has been compared with Discriminatory AI, which cooperates only with cooperators. Samaritan AI sustains more cooperation under weak selection, where it works mainly as a cooperative role model, and Discriminatory AI under strong selection, where the payoff advantage it gives cooperators carries more weight ([[zimmaroEmergenceCooperationOneshot2024|Zimmaro2024]]). Role asymmetry changes this balance. In an ultimatum game between separate proposer and receiver populations, agents that always offer a fair split fail to make human receivers demand one, whereas agents that always reject unfair offers bring fairness to both populations. A discriminatory proposer, which offers a fair split only to receivers who would reject less, outperforms both, especially under strong selection, and needs fewer agents to do so ([[songEvolutionFairnessHybrid2026|Song2026]]). The public goods game shows the same contrast, as agents made to always cooperate leave the synergy needed for human cooperation unchanged, while agents that mirror the behavior of the humans they play with lower it ([[hintzePromotingCooperationPublic2026|Hintze2026]]).

A smaller group of models takes the next step in this progression and lets the agents learn from past interactions. Agents that learn by reinforcement, making an action more likely when its outcome meets their aspiration level and less likely otherwise, raise human cooperation in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma even as a minority. These agents judge outcomes by a utility that weighs their co-players' payoffs against their own. The effect is largest for agents that balance the two payoffs, and purely self-regarding or purely altruistic agents reduce cooperation ([[guoEngineeringOptimalCooperation2024|Guo2024]]). The reverse arrangement has also been modeled. On a lattice, Q-learning machines play a repeated prisoner's dilemma with humans who hold fixed reactive strategies. The machines learn conditional cooperation when these strategies are tolerant, as with generous tit-for-tat or win-stay, lose-shift, and what they learn is shaped by the human strategies they face ([[quanHumanMachineCooperation2026|Quan2026]]).

<!--- Large scale, human-only --->

Beyond reinforcement learning, the update rules and reasoning mechanisms of this progression have mostly been studied in populations with no artificial agents, where the rule itself is the object of study. The traditional assumption, also made for the humans in the hybrid models above, is payoff-based imitation, in which individuals copy more successful peers. Research on social learning shows, however, that copying is rarely indiscriminate, as people are selective about what, when and whom they copy, and switch flexibly between several heuristics ([[kendalSocialLearningStrategies2018|Kendal2018]]).

One alternative is conformity, the copying of the most common strategy instead of the most successful. Added to payoff-based learning, its effect depends on the game. It can make cooperation stable in a prisoner's dilemma and shifts the basins of attraction in a stag hunt, although under cultural group selection it hinders cooperation overall ([[mollemanEffectsConformismCultural2013|Molleman2013]]). On networks, an appropriate fraction of conformists helps cooperative clusters expand beyond what network reciprocity alone allows ([[szolnokiConformityEnhancesNetwork2015|Szolnoki2015]]), provided it is the masses who conform, since making the most influential individuals (or leaders) conform is worse than assigning conformity at random ([[szolnokiLeadersShouldNot2016|Szolnoki2016]]). Conformity triggered only when individuals are dissatisfied with their payoffs also promotes cooperation in spatial public goods games ([[quanRationalConformityBehavior2022|Quan2022]]).

A second alternative is aspiration, in which individuals compare their payoff with a level they aspire to and need no information about others. Even in well-mixed populations it can favor a different strategy than imitation does, as soon as games involve more than two players ([[duAspirationDynamicsMultiplayer2014|Du2014]]). When individuals mix the two rules, relying more on aspiration promotes cooperation in well-mixed populations, while inhibiting it in structured ones in prisoner's dilemma and coexistence games, and does the reverse in coordination games ([[wangEvolutionaryGameDynamics2019|Wang2019]]).

Deliberative mechanisms are the last step, and consist in weighing alternative courses of action and inferring what others intend. Counterfactual thinking, in which individuals weigh what they would have earned had they acted otherwise, has been modeled as a myopic best response. A small share of counterfactual thinkers is enough to lift a population of social learners to high cooperation in collective dilemmas that require coordination, with little effect where no coordination is needed ([[pereiraCounterfactualThinkingCooperation2019|Pereira2019]]). However, the benefit is not monotonic in their prevalence, as it depends on the coordination structure of the game, and it does not extend to cooperation among the counterfactual thinkers themselves ([[fernandesCounterfactualThinkingStochastic2024|Fernandes2024]]). Inferring what others intend is the domain of theory of mind, formalized as recursive reasoning of bounded depth, where the depth defines an individual's sophistication ([[yoshidaGameTheoryMind2008|Yoshida2008]]). Population models have studied it mainly as a trait under selection, which we discuss under cognitive evolution. Across these mechanisms, richer reasoning does not raise cooperation in any uniform way, as its effect depends on the game, on the population structure and on how many individuals use it.

<!--- ===== Cognitive Evolution ===== -->

Finally, we turn to cognitive evolution, that is, whether the cognitive profiles described above are fixed or themselves change over time. Most evolutionary models let selection act on behavior and take the process that generates it as given ([[hanSocialPhysicsAge2026|Han2026]]). A separate line of work makes the reasoning mechanism itself the trait under selection. When agents can either respond intuitively or pay a cost to deliberate and tailor their behavior to the type of game they face, selection favors either intuitive defectors or agents who cooperate intuitively and deliberate in order to defect in one-shot games. It never favors deliberating in order to cooperate (Bear2016). Update rules themselves have also been placed under selection. When strategies and update rules co-evolve in a weak prisoner's dilemma on networks, a single rule comes to dominate, and cooperation is generally higher than under a fixed rule (Cardillo2010). Cognition and cooperation can also reinforce each other, since cooperation, once it exists, favors enhanced cognition, which in turn increases the returns from cooperating and stabilizes cooperation as relatedness falls ([[dossantosCoevolutionCooperationCognition2018|dos Santos2018]]).

Theory of mind has been studied in the same way, with individuals carrying a level of reasoning and selection determining which levels spread. More sophistication is not simply better. An informational cost keeps highly sophisticated individuals from fully exploiting less sophisticated ones in competitive settings, and cooperative settings favor lower levels of sophistication (Devaine2014). In a sequential dilemma, intermediate levels of reasoning evolve, provided individuals make reasoning mistakes and face a temptation for higher future gains (Lenaerts2024). Whether theory of mind is selected at all depends on the environment. Inferring a partner's prosociality gives no advantage in the games usually studied, only where partners are stable and payoffs vary (Qi2022), in line with the argument that attributing preferences to others is an adaptation to strategic environments with persistent novelty (Kimbrough2013). In such varied environments, a reciprocal strategy built on Bayesian theory of mind outcompetes classic automata such as tit-for-tat and sustains cooperation across more settings (Kleiman-Weiner2025). Strategies that recognize a co-player's intentions likewise promote cooperation (Han2011), most effectively when they propose a costly commitment only if their prediction is not confident enough (Han2015).

Cognitive profiles also change through learning and cultural transmission. In a laboratory experiment with migration and changing environments, people relied more on conformist social learning after spatial than after temporal changes, and their reliance on social learning declined quickly in the rounds after migration ([[deffnerDynamicSocialLearning2020|Deffner2020]]). In a large transmission-chain experiment, complex problem-solving algorithms persisted across generations when participants could choose whom to learn from, and frequently went extinct otherwise, replaced by simpler, lower-performing ones ([[thompsonComplexCognitiveAlgorithms2022|Thompson2022]]). What evolves can also be the content of cognition. When each individual holds a network of interacting beliefs, adoption depends on internal coherence as well as on social conformity, and a small number of individuals with highly coherent beliefs can overturn a consensus ([[rodriguezCollectiveDynamicsBelief2016|Rodriguez2016]]).

For artificial agents the route of change is different. Humans acquire their strategies from those around them, whereas AI systems are trained on human-generated data and selected through human feedback and adoption, and in turn shape the behavior and the data of the people who use them ([[hanSocialPhysicsAge2026|Han2026]], [[pedreschiHumanAICoevolution2025|Pedreschi2025]]). Language models have also become a channel of cultural transmission in their own right, influencing which cultural traits are generated, passed on and selected ([[hanSocialPhysicsAge2026|Han2026]]). Simulations with language-model agents have begun to put cognitively rich agents under selection. In a simulated hunter-gatherer society, agents with universal and reciprocal moral dispositions were the most stable, selfish agents were strongly disfavored, and the cost of moral judgment shifted which type prevailed ([[zihengWhyAreWe2025|Ziheng2025]]). In the hybrid population models reviewed above, by contrast, cognitive profiles are set by design, as humans follow a single update rule, usually imitation, and agents follow either a fixed policy or a learning rule that changes their behavior but not the rule itself.

## Research Gap

The literature reviewed previously has established that artificial agents modify human cooperation, in ways that are contingent on the structure of the population, on the dynamics of the interaction and on the design of the agents. However, it remains scarce with respect to how the cognitive profiles of artificial agents affect the emergence and stability of cooperation in large-scale hybrid societies, and to how these profiles themselves evolve over time.

The two bodies of work that come closest stop on opposite sides. Studies of dyads and small teams involve agents that communicate, adapt and reason, but not the population dynamics through which cooperation emerges or collapses. 

Population-scale models abstract most of these dimensions away. The hybrid models reviewed below do not represent embodiment or affective skills. Affect has been modeled in human-only populations, where expressing emotions supports the evolution of cooperation under indirect reciprocity, particularly when errors are frequent (Correia da Fonseca2025), and where the social manifestation of guilt leads to stable cooperation (Pereira2017). Identity, in turn, enters as the agent's kind and its visibility to others, as discussed above.


Population-scale models capture those dynamics, but give artificial agents a fixed policy ([[sharmaSmallBotsBig2023|Sharma2023]], [[guoFacilitatingCooperationHumanagent2023|Guo2023]], [[zimmaroEmergenceCooperationOneshot2024|Zimmaro2024]], [[songEvolutionFairnessHybrid2026|Song2026]]) or, at most, a reinforcement-learning rule ([[guoEngineeringOptimalCooperation2024|Guo2024]], [[quanHumanMachineCooperation2026|Quan2026]]), and give humans a single update rule.

The richer profiles of the progression have been studied only in populations without artificial agents, and one mechanism at a time. This is the case for heuristic update rules such as conformity ([[szolnokiConformityEnhancesNetwork2015|Szolnoki2015]]) and aspiration ([[duAspirationDynamicsMultiplayer2014|Du2014]]), and for deliberative mechanisms such as counterfactual thinking ([[pereiraCounterfactualThinkingCooperation2019|Pereira2019]], [[fernandesCounterfactualThinkingStochastic2024|Fernandes2024]]) and theory of mind (Devaine2014, Lenaerts2024). The same holds for the evolution of cognition, which has been modeled as a general trait ([[dossantosCoevolutionCooperationCognition2018|dos Santos2018]]), for single mechanisms (Bear2016, Lenaerts2024) and for beliefs ([[rodriguezCollectiveDynamicsBelief2016|Rodriguez2016]]) in human-only populations, and in simulations populated entirely by language-model agents ([[zihengWhyAreWe2025|Ziheng2025]]).

The gap is therefore twofold. First, no population-scale model examines how artificial agents with heuristic or deliberative cognitive profiles affect the emergence and stability of cooperation among humans, or compares these profiles within a single framework. Second, no hybrid model allows these profiles to change over time. A recent roadmap identifies the same limitation, noting that existing models assume homogeneous populations and do not account for hybrid societies in which humans interact with artificial agents endowed with distinct cognitive architectures ([[hanSocialPhysicsAge2026|Han2026]]).
