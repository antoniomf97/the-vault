## 1. The update schedule

**The claim, in one sentence:** when individuals revise (all at once or one at a time, in which order, after how many interactions) can decide whether cooperation evolves, and in a hybrid population the two kinds need not revise on the same clock.

This is 29 Sep decision 7 ("when it gets to revise goes under *when*"), now with Rui behind it.

**Evidence (verified this session):**

- *Synchronous vs asynchronous.* Huberman & Glance 1993: the spatial cooperation results differ substantially between discrete and continuous time; with sequential updating, the baseline spatial PD sustains no cooperation at all (Izquierdo, Izquierdo & Sandholm's summary). Grilo & Correia 2011: asynchrony generally *helps* cooperation and hurts it only when individuals imitate their most successful neighbour with high probability; the shift from synchronous to sequential is mostly smooth and monotonic.
- *The order inside asynchronous updating* (Rui's "birth-death"). Ohtsuki et al. 2006: on graphs, death-birth favours cooperation when b/c > k; under birth-death, "selection does not favour cooperation". Zukewich et al. 2013: the two differ only in the order of two events. Hindersin & Traulsen 2015: the same random graphs amplify selection under birth-death and suppress it under death-birth.
- *Without structure the schedule matters less, but the clock still matters.* For weak selection the same condition (the one-third law) holds under the Moran process (Nowak et al. 2004) and under Wright-Fisher, its synchronous counterpart (Imhof & Nowak 2006; I know this only through Lessard 2007, so read it). Roca et al. 2009: the update rule matters most in structured populations, and on a complete network the differences "may be not relevant". But Roca, Cuesta & Sánchez 2006, in a well-mixed Moran process: when few interactions happen between revisions, the stag hunt moves toward the inefficient equilibrium and the snowdrift loses its coexistence state. Those are two of your three games.
- *Two kinds, two clocks.* Bergstrom & Lachmann 2003: in a mutualism, the slower-evolving species can secure the larger share (Red King). Gokhale & Traulsen 2012: in multiplayer games this can reverse. The relative rate matters; its sign depends on the game.
- *What the hybrid models do.* Every one I could check revises asynchronously, one individual per step: [[bookerDiscriminatorySamaritanWhich2023|Booker2023]] (the arXiv version of [[zimmaroEmergenceCooperationOneshot2024|Zimmaro2024]]: "only one individual is eligible for imitation at each time step"), [[terruchaArtCompensationHow2024|Terrucha2024]] (one adaptive agent per step), [[jiaAsymmetricInteractionPreference2025|Jia2025]] (asynchronous Monte Carlo, humans and agents at the same average rate). The agents either never revise or revise on the humans' clock. [[quanHumanMachineCooperation2026|Quan2026]] is the reverse arrangement: machines learn, humans' strategies are fixed. Check Sharma2023, Guo2023, Song2026, Fu2026 and Guo2024 against the PDFs before writing "all".

**The sentence that ties it to the chapter.** Grilo & Correia's condition (near-deterministic imitation) is a close relative of the moderator that already flips the effect of agents in four hybrid models ([[sharmaSmallBotsBig2023|Sharma2023]], [[guoFacilitatingCooperationHumanagent2023|Guo2023]], Zimmaro2024, [[guoEngineeringOptimalCooperation2024|Guo2024]]): how closely imitation follows payoff. Without that link the paragraph is a methods aside again.

**What leaves:**
- Koster2024 → [[Framework]], method. One sentence: revising one individual per event is the embedded chain of a continuous-time process, so the fixed-time-step problem Koster warns about doesn't arise. That also closes the 6 Oct obligation.
- The horizon sentence → game protocols. One-shot vs repeated is part of the game.

**Trap.** Your model is well mixed. The spatial results show that the schedule has leverage; the ones that bind *your* model are Roca 2006 (interactions per revision) and the relative clock. If the paragraph doesn't end there, "deviated" comes back.

**Why it isn't a detour.** It is what the RQ3 rule will stand on. Terrucha2024 justifies fixed agents by saying AI products are rule-based and "learning on the fly might be costly or even dangerous". Deployed agents change when a new version ships: every copy at once, rarely. Humans revise one at a time, all the time. Synchronous and slow against asynchronous and continuous is a candidate answer to comment 4. That claim is yours, so it goes in the Framework, not the review.

## 2. How the studies tell humans and AI apart

**The claim:** experiments tell the kinds apart by what participants are told; models tell them apart by who is allowed to revise. Neither tells them apart by how they reason.

That is the 28 Aug diagnosis ("AI is not a distinct species") stated as a fact about the literature that anyone can check.

**Experiments.** The "AI" is a program whose behaviour the experimenter sets, plus a label. The informative designs cross the two: [[ishowo-olokoBehaviouralEvidenceTransparency2019|Ishowo-Oloko2019]] (true or false identity: bots induce more cooperation than humans until disclosed), [[mutznerBoundedNormativeEquivalence2026|Mutzner2026]] (the same bot strategies framed as human or as AI: no label effect under anonymous aggregate feedback). Label effects on their own: [[karpusAlgorithmExploitationHumans2021|Karpus2021]], Barreda-Tarrazona2026, [[jiangHumansLearnPrefer2025|Jiang2025]].

**Models.** The 5 Oct table, extended today:

| What separates the kinds | Where |
| --- | --- |
| **Who revises.** The agent's behaviour is set from outside and never revised; only humans evolve | Sharma2023, Guo2023, Zimmaro2024, [[songEvolutionFairnessHybrid2026\|Song2026]], [[santosEvolutionCollectiveFairness2019\|Santos2019]], Terrucha2024, [[fuOptimalIntegrationIntelligent2026\|Fu2026]], Pires & Santos 2025. Only Terrucha2024 justifies the choice (rule-based products; learning on the fly is risky) |
| **How they revise.** A different rule | Guo2024 (reinforcement learning against imitation); Jia2025 (both imitate, but agents play one strategy against every neighbour, humans one per neighbour); Quan2026 (Q-learning machines against fixed human strategies) |
| **Who learns from whom** | Humans can copy agents: Sharma2023, Guo2023 (under pairwise comparison), Zimmaro2024, Song2026. Agents are never role models: Fu2026 (base model), Pires & Santos 2025 (main model) |
| **Whether the kind is seen, and used** | Zimmaro2024 (humans imitate AI with a separate intensity); Jia2025 (identity visible, later at a cost; a preference for one kind); Pires & Santos 2025 (agents judged by a different norm; their assessments coordinated, humans' private) |
| **What they want** | Guo2024 (utility weighs co-players' payoffs); Santos2019 (hard-coded prosocial) |
| **Where they sit** | Guo2023 (hubs or random nodes); Song2026 (proposers or receivers) |
| **Never** | Memory, cost of reasoning, speed of revision (5 Oct). Information appears once: Pires & Santos's coordinated assessments |

**Say this before a reviewer does.** An individual whose policy is set from outside and never revised is what evolutionary dynamics calls a *zealot*. Masuda 2012: a small share of zealous cooperators makes a population largely cooperative unless selection is very strong. That is the "cooperative bots help under weak imitation" result, with no AI in it. Jia2025 itself calls the fixed agents of earlier work "committed agents", and Dong, Guo, Shen & Shi 2026 study zealots in N-person stag hunts (Guo and Shen are authors of Guo2023 and Sharma2023). In the fixed-policy models, "AI" is an interpretation of a zealot, and the AI-specific ingredients are the few in rows 2–6.

Say it as description, not criticism. Those papers asked design questions (which behaviour, how many agents, where), and for those the label is harmless. It becomes a limitation only for your question, because a zealot does not reason.

**Where it goes.** At the head of *intrinsic nature*, where the reader first meets the kind. The paragraph then keeps only what shows the label matters (Guzman2020, Mou2017, Ishowo-Oloko2019, Jiang2025, Shirado2017/2020, Zimmaro2024), shorter. Pay for the table by deleting the "who revises" clauses in agent design; the table holds them now.

**What it buys.** The first half of the gap needs no more argument: no row of the table is cognitive. Leave the gap sentence alone (closed 4 Oct); the table supports it.

**For the Framework.** Pires & Santos 2025 is the precedent the 6 Oct sheet lacked for "agents global, humans local": agents whose assessments are coordinated while humans keep private ones. Read the paper before leaning on it.

## 3. Institutions

**What's wrong now.** The paragraph says institutions "bind humans and artificial agents alike" and cites [[bartelheimerConceptualizingHybridIntelligent2025|Bartelheimer2025]]. Its abstract says the institutional arrangements of hybrid ecosystems *differ* from earlier ones because of hybrid agency. And no hybrid study in the review treats the two kinds alike. Rui's question is aimed at that sentence.

**The claim:** institutions do not treat the two kinds alike. In the work reviewed, the agent sits under the institution with a rule of its own, acts as the institution, or is regulated through the humans who make it.

| The agent's position | Studies | How the kinds are separated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under the institution, with its own rule | [[hintzePromotingCooperationPublic2026\|Hintze2026]] (a policy imposed on the agents: mandatory cooperation, player-controlled cooperation, or mimicking players); Pires & Santos 2025 (indirect reciprocity) | The rule is written into the agent, or a norm judges it differently. Humans are reached through payoffs |
| As the institution | McKee et al. 2023 (a deep-RL social planner recommends ties: 77.7% cooperation against 42.8% in static networks); [[shiradoNetworkEngineeringUsing2020\|Shirado2020]] (bots that rewire); Koster et al. 2022, Democratic AI (RL designs a redistribution mechanism that sanctions free riders and wins a majority vote) | The agent sets or enforces the rules; only humans are subject to them |
| Regulated through humans | Han, Pereira, Santos & Lenaerts 2020 (an AI race whose players are the ones building AI); Alalawi et al. 2026 (users, AI creators and regulators; the AI system is what is built and trusted, not a player); [[hanSocialPhysicsAge2026\|Han2026]], sixth direction | The agent is not a player; incentives fall on its makers |

Real institutions take the third route and make the separation itself a rule. Article 50(1) of the EU AI Act obliges *providers* to design systems that interact with people so that people are told they are dealing with AI. It applies from 2 Aug 2026, and the AI Omnibus did not move it. California's SB 1001 (operative 1 July 2019) makes it unlawful to use a bot that misleads about its artificial identity to sell or to sway a vote. AI is not a legal person, so duties fall on providers; Bryson, Diamantis & Grant 2017 argue why that should stay so. Proposals go further: identifiers for agents (Chan et al. 2024) and credentials for humans (Adler et al. 2024).

**Why it matters to you:**
- The disclosure rule is the variable Ishowo-Oloko2019 and Mutzner2026 manipulate, and their results diverge with the information structure. One sentence linking them turns the institutions paragraph into a finding.
- Since 2 Aug 2026, the sheet's "kind visible to all" is the legal default in the EU for agents that talk to people. That is a one-sentence justification for the assumption in [[Framework]].
- *Yours to defend or drop:* an institution changes payoffs, and payoffs change behaviour only through an update rule. A sanction cannot move a fixed agent, moves a conformist only by changing frequencies, and moves an aspiration learner directly. Which levers reach an agent depends on its profile. That is one more reason profiles matter, and it lets institutions sit in your Limitations with an argument rather than a shrug.

**Watch.** On 29 Sep we separated institutions (an entity that acts: rewards, punishes, matches, spends a budget) from norms (shared rules enforced by the players). By that definition indirect reciprocity is a norm, and Pires & Santos fits under institutions only if their coordinated agents are read as a public reputation system. Choose the definition and apply it to Pires.
